1. Foundations of Proof

A statement that is either true or false — proof is the art of combining propositions with connectives and tracking how the truth values are forced.

A proof that takes the hypothesis as given and reaches the conclusion by a straight chain of justified steps — no detours.

Splitting the domain into cases that cover everything, and proving the claim in each case — together the cases cover the whole proof.

∀\forall∀ (for all) and ∃\exists∃ (there exists) — the symbols that pin down precisely how a statement ranges over its domain. Negation swaps the two.

The contrapositive of P⇒QP \Rightarrow QP⇒Q is ¬Q⇒¬P\lnot Q \Rightarrow \lnot P¬Q⇒¬P — always equivalent to the original, so it can be proved in its place when the direct route stalls.

The method that assumes the negation of the conclusion and derives a contradiction — if assuming ¬P\lnot P¬P yields a contradiction (⊥\bot⊥), then PPP holds.

Proof by Contradiction

P  ⟺  QP \iff QP⟺Q — an "if and only if" claim that is completed only by proving the two directions P⇒QP \Rightarrow QP⇒Q and Q⇒PQ \Rightarrow PQ⇒P separately.

Induction that assumes all of P(2),…,P(n−1)P(2), \dots, P(n-1)P(2),…,P(n−1) when proving P(n)P(n)P(n) — used when the step needs much smaller values, like the factors of nnn.

A relation that is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive — its equivalence classes partition the whole set without overlap.
