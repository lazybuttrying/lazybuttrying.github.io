Glossary

Key terms from the scenes, grouped by chapter. Each term links to the scene where it is introduced.

1. The Macro Lens

Gross domestic product (GDP)¶

The market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a period. By expenditure, GDP=C+I+G+NXGDP = C + I + G + NXGDP=C+I+G+NX.

The Circle Money Travels: GDP

Output gap¶

How far actual output sits from potential output. A negative gap is a slump; a positive gap is overheating.

One Shock, Three Schools

2. Aggregate Demand and Supply

Aggregate demand¶

Total spending demanded at each price level. Unlike a micro demand curve, it slopes down because a lower price level raises real money balances, lowers interest rates, and depreciates the currency.

Why Aggregate Demand Slopes Down

Aggregate supply¶

Total output supplied at each price level. Upward-sloping in the short run because some prices are sticky; vertical at potential output in the long run.

After the Shock

3. Spending, the Multiplier, and the Paradox of Thrift

Multiplier¶

How many units of income one unit of spending creates. With marginal propensity to consume ccc, the government-spending multiplier is 11−c\frac{1}{1-c}1−c1​.

The Multiplier: Spending That Echoes

Paradox of thrift¶

When everyone tries to save more, incomes fall and actual saving does not rise — it can even fall. The classic fallacy of composition: a virtue for one household, a trap for all.

The Paradox of Thrift: When Everyone Saves, No One Does

4. Money and Prices

Price level¶

The average level of prices overall. A micro price is an exchange ratio between two goods (a relative price); the price level is the ruler for nominal values themselves.

A Price Is a Ratio; the Price Level Is a Level

Money neutrality¶

In the long run, the money supply changes only prices, not real output or employment. It follows from the quantity theory and pairs with short-run non-neutrality.

The Equation of Exchange and Neutrality

Quantity theory of money¶

Starting from the identity MV=PYMV = PYMV=PY (money × velocity = price level × real output), the theory that long-run inflation comes from money growth.

The Equation of Exchange and Neutrality

5. Where the Interest Rate Is Decided

Loanable funds market¶

The classical market that sets the interest rate: saving supplies funds, investment demands them, and the interest rate is the price of saved funds.

The Market for Saved Funds

Crowding out¶

Government borrowing pushes the interest rate up and displaces private investment, as the deficit absorbs saving in the loanable funds market.

The Market for Saved Funds

Liquidity preference¶

Keynes's theory of the interest rate: people hold money for transactions, precaution, and speculation, and the rate is set where money demand meets money supply.

The Market for Money

6. The Two Faces of Wages, and Unemployment

Menu cost¶

The fixed cost of changing a price, named after reprinting restaurant menus. Small — but near the optimal price the loss from not adjusting is also small, so prices stick.

Menu Costs: When Prices Won't Move

7. The Open Economy and Trade

Trade balance identity¶

Net exports always equal saving minus investment (NX=S−INX = S - INX=S−I). A trade deficit is therefore a saving-investment fact, not something a tariff can fix.

The Trade Balance Is Saving minus Investment

Real exchange rate¶

The exchange ratio between two countries' goods. It is the price that moves to keep the trade balance identity true — doing the adjusting that tariffs cannot.

The Exchange Rate Closes the Loop

8. Growth: Solow and Beyond

Steady state¶

In the Solow model, the capital stock where saving exactly offsets depreciation. Growth returns to zero there; only the level remains.

A Country Cannot Grow on Machines Alone

Bellman equation¶

The core of dynamic programming: today's value splits into the payoff of today's choice plus the value of tomorrow, V(a)=max⁡[u(c)+βV(a′)]V(a) = \max [u(c) + \beta V(a')]V(a)=max[u(c)+βV(a′)].

Where the Saving Rate Comes From

9. Risk and Uncertainty

Expected utility¶

How a person evaluates a risky choice: by expected utility ∑piu(xi)\sum p_i u(x_i)∑pi​u(xi​), not expected money. A curved utility function is what creates risk aversion and the demand for insurance.

Expected Utility vs Expected Profit

Precautionary saving¶

Extra saving held against an uncertain future income. It grows with uncertainty — and when everyone does it at once, aggregate demand falls and a slump can follow.

When Uncertainty Becomes the Recession
